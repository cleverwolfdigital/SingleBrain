You cannot reset what you have not measured.

That sounds obvious. It isn't, because almost nobody does it. The standard move, when life feels
heavy, is to jump straight to solutions — a new app, a new morning routine, a new calendar system,
a new commitment made at eleven at night with real conviction and no evidence behind it. You skip
the diagnosis and go straight to the prescription.

Then it fails, and you conclude you lack discipline.

You don't lack discipline. You lack a baseline. You built a system to fix a problem you never
actually located, and it solved something that wasn't broken while the real leak kept running.

The audit is how you stop guessing. It is the least glamorous chapter in this book and the one that
determines whether everything after it works. Chapter 1 established that overload comes from
decision volume, not from character. Chapter 2 established that the systems worth building are
simple, visible, and automated. This chapter tells you where to point them.

Give it ninety minutes. Once.

---

## Why self-assessment usually fails

Before the method, the three ways this goes wrong — because you have probably done all three.

**You audit by mood.** You assess your life on a Sunday night when you're tired, or on the first
morning of a vacation when you're not. Both readings are garbage. Mood doesn't just color the
score, it changes which domains you even look at. Tired-you audits health. Anxious-you audits
money. Neither sees the whole board.

**You audit by narrative.** Asked how your health is, you don't check anything — you retrieve the
story you tell about your health. "I've been pretty good lately." That sentence is a summary of a
summary, three revisions removed from any fact, and it's shaped more by what you want to be true
than by what is. Narrative is how you *explain* your life. It is not data about your life.

**You audit vaguely.** "Work is stressful." That is not a finding. You can't build against it,
can't tell whether it improved, and can't tell whether the stress is volume, ambiguity, conflict,
or a commute. Vague findings feel like insight and produce nothing.

A real audit defeats all three by refusing to ask you how you feel. It asks you what is
observably there.

## The three rules

### Rule 1 — Evidence over feeling

Every score in this audit must point at something you could show another person: a bank statement,
a calendar, a message thread, a photo, a step count, a bill. If you cannot cite it, you cannot
score it.

This is not because feelings don't matter. It's because feelings are the *output* you're trying to
improve, and using the output as the measurement makes the whole thing circular. You feel scattered;
you rate yourself scattered; you have learned nothing.

Look at the artifacts instead. Your calendar, your bank account, and your camera roll are three of
the most honest witnesses you own. None of them are trying to protect your self-image.

### Rule 2 — Snapshot over story

You are recording where things stand **today**. Not the arc. Not where they're heading. Not why.

The instant you start explaining a score — *"it's low because of the quarter I just had"* — you've
stopped auditing and started defending. Write the number. The explanation can wait for the next
chapter, where it becomes useful. Here it's just interference.

There's a practical reason too: the reset is meant to be repeatable. A snapshot can be compared to
the next snapshot. A story can't be compared to anything.

### Rule 3 — Fast enough to repeat

If your audit takes a weekend, you will do it once, feel accomplished, and never do it again. That
makes it a ritual, not an instrument.

Design constraint: **ninety minutes the first time, twenty minutes every time after.** The first
run is slower only because you're locating the evidence. Once you know where to look, it's a short
sweep. If your version is taking longer than that, you are explaining rather than recording — see
Rule 2.

Simple, visible, repeatable. The same three properties the systems have. The audit is the first
system you build, and it is built the same way as all the others.

## Part 1 — The Decision Load Inventory

Start here, before the domains, because it measures the thing this book is actually about.

Chapter 1's claim was that overload is a volume problem: you're making thousands of decisions a day
with no structure carrying any of the weight. This inventory tests that claim against your specific
life and tells you where the volume is concentrated.

Take a blank page. For twenty minutes, list every **open loop** — anything unresolved that your
brain is currently holding. Not tasks you've written down somewhere safe. The ones you're *carrying*.

Prompts, to shake them loose:

- What did you think about at 3 a.m. this month?
- What are you avoiding opening — an inbox, a folder, a drawer, an app?
- What decision have you deferred more than three times?
- What would go wrong if you were unreachable for two weeks?
- What do other people have to ask you about because only you know it?
- What recurring choice do you re-litigate every single time instead of having decided once?

That last one matters most. There's a difference between a decision you make once and a decision
you re-make daily. What to eat. Whether to work out. Which of six things to do first. Whether to
answer that message now. Each re-make is cheap; the volume is what breaks you.

Now sort the list into three columns:

| Column | Definition | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Decided** | Resolved. Just needs executing. | Not load. Cross it off. |
| **Deciding** | Genuinely open. Needs a real call. | Real load, but finite and workable. |
| **Re-deciding** | You settle it, then reopen it. Repeatedly. | **This is the leak.** |

Count each column.

The **Re-deciding** count is your single most important number in this entire audit. It is decision
load made visible. Every item in that column is a choice consuming energy on a loop, forever, until
a system absorbs it.

Most people, doing this honestly the first time, land somewhere between eight and twenty. If you're
above twenty, that is not a discipline failure — it is a structural one, and it is exactly what the
rest of this book dismantles.

Write the three counts down. You will compare them at your next reset.

## Part 2 — The Five Domain Sweep

Five domains: **Health, Wealth, Relationships, Work, Energy.**

For each one you'll do the same three steps: pull the evidence, answer three questions, assign two
scores. Roughly ten minutes per domain.

A note on why *Energy* is separate from *Health*, since it's the pairing people question. Health is
the state of the machine. Energy is the fuel available to you today. They come apart constantly —
you can be medically fine and running on empty, or carrying a real health problem while feeling
sharp. Collapsing them hides the most common failure mode in this book's audience: a body that's
technically fine attached to a week with nothing left in it.

### Health

**Evidence to pull:** last thirty days. Sleep data if you have it. Step count or training log.
Photos of what you ate — your camera roll is more honest than your memory. Any appointment you've
postponed. Prescriptions, and whether you're actually taking them.

**The three questions:**

1. What did I do for my body in the last thirty days that I could prove to someone?
2. What is the longest streak I broke, and when?
3. What have I been postponing that has a date attached — a check-up, a scan, a referral?

**Common finding:** the problem is almost never knowledge. You know what to do. The problem is that
health decisions are made at the exact moments you have the least capacity to make them — late,
hungry, tired, mid-crisis.

### Wealth

**Evidence to pull:** last ninety days of transactions across every account. Current balances. Every
recurring subscription. Your actual debt figure — the number, not the range you'd say out loud.

**The three questions:**

1. Do I know, to the nearest hundred, what came in and what went out last month?
2. What am I paying for that I haven't used in ninety days?
3. If income stopped today, how many weeks could I run?

**Common finding:** the gap isn't between what you earn and what you need. It's between what you
think you spend and what you spend. That gap is invisible by design — it's made of small,
automated, individually reasonable outflows. Which is also, note, the shape of a *system*. Money
leaves your life on autopilot. Almost nothing enters it that way. That asymmetry is worth sitting
with.

### Relationships

**Evidence to pull:** your messages, your call log, your calendar. Who did you actually see, not
who do you feel close to.

**The three questions:**

1. Who did I have real contact with in the last thirty days — and who did I only think about?
2. Who reached out and got a slow reply or none?
3. Which relationship is running purely on history right now, with no current input?

**Common finding:** this domain degrades silently and has no alarm. Health complains. Money
complains. Work complains loudly and on a schedule. Relationships just quietly thin out, and the
first real signal usually arrives well after the damage. Being the last domain to alert makes it
the first one to neglect — which is exactly why it needs a system rather than good intentions.

### Work

**Evidence to pull:** last thirty days of calendar. Your actual project list. Anything with a
deadline you've moved.

**The three questions:**

1. What percentage of my week went to work only I can do?
2. What is on my list that has been on my list for more than sixty days?
3. What am I the single point of failure for?

**Common finding:** the load isn't hours, it's *fragmentation* — the number of distinct contexts
you switch between. Ten hours on one thing is survivable. Ten hours across nine things is not, and
it produces a week that feels enormous and shows almost nothing for it.

That sixty-day question is doing quiet work, by the way. Anything sitting that long isn't a task
you haven't got to. It's a decision you haven't made, wearing a task costume.

### Energy

**Evidence to pull:** the last two weeks, honestly recalled. When you were sharp. When you were
useless. What preceded each.

**The three questions:**

1. What time of day am I reliably at my best — and what am I currently spending it on?
2. What specific activity or person consistently costs me a disproportionate amount?
3. When did I last have genuine unstructured time, and what did I do with it?

**Common finding:** most people spend their best hours on other people's priorities and their worst
hours on their own. The morning goes to inbox and response; the ambition gets whatever's left at
nine at night. Nothing about that is a motivation problem. It's a sequencing problem, and it's
fixable.

## Scoring: Floor and Trend

Two numbers per domain. Not one — one number flattens two very different situations into the same
score and loses the thing you most need to know.

**Floor (1–5):** how bad is this at its worst, right now?

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 | Actively failing. Causing damage this week. |
| 2 | Fragile. One bad week from a real problem. |
| 3 | Functional. Nothing's broken. Nothing's built either. |
| 4 | Solid. Holds up under normal pressure. |
| 5 | Systematised. Holds up without your attention. |

**Trend (−1 / 0 / +1):** which direction has this moved over ninety days? Down, flat, or up.

Score the floor from evidence, not from the best week you can remember. The floor is the point of
the measure — a domain that's excellent when you're on and collapses when you're not is a domain
running on your attention, and your attention is the resource this whole book is trying to protect.

## Part 3 — The one-page snapshot

Everything above compresses to one page. Date it. That date is the whole point — it makes this
comparable to the next one, and comparability is what turns an exercise into an instrument.

```
CURRENT STATE — [DATE]

DECISION LOAD
  Decided:      __
  Deciding:     __
  Re-deciding:  __   ← the number that matters

DOMAINS                    Floor   Trend
  Health                     _      __
  Wealth                     _      __
  Relationships              _      __
  Work                       _      __
  Energy                     _      __

LOWEST FLOOR:        ______________
STEEPEST DECLINE:    ______________
TOP RE-DECIDED LOOP: ______________

NEXT RESET DUE:      ______________
```

Keep it where you'll see it. Not in a folder, not in an app you open twice a year — visible, in the
same place you'll keep everything else this book asks you to build. Visibility was one of the three
properties in Chapter 2, and it applies to the audit before it applies to anything else. An audit
you filed is an audit you have already wasted.

## Reading it: find the constraint, not the list

Here's where most people ruin a perfectly good audit.

You now have five domains and a decision-load count. If any of it was honest, several of those
numbers are bad. The instinct — powerful, and wrong — is to fix all of them. Monday morning, new
regime, five domains, total overhaul.

That fails. Reliably. And it fails for the reason this book exists: **a five-front reset is a
massive decision-load increase, deployed against a person who just measured their decision load and
found it too high.** You'd be treating the disease with more of the disease.

So don't read the snapshot as a to-do list. Read it as a search for one thing: **the constraint** —
the single domain whose failure is generating symptoms in the others.

Domains are not independent, and they fail in fairly predictable chains:

- **Energy collapses → everything else follows.** Health slips because workouts need fuel, work
  fragments because focus needs fuel, relationships thin because presence needs fuel. If Energy is
  your floor, treat almost every other low score as downstream noise until proven otherwise.
- **Wealth pressure → Work distortion → Relationship cost.** Money stress makes you take work you
  shouldn't, which takes hours you don't have, which come out of people who don't complain.
- **Work fragmentation → Health erosion.** Context-switching doesn't just consume the workday, it
  consumes the recovery around it. Meals get skipped, sleep gets shortened, exercise is first out.

To find your constraint, ask: **which of these low scores would still be low if the others were
fixed?**

That one is the constraint. The others are symptoms wearing costumes.

Circle it. That domain, alone, is the target of everything in the next chapter. One domain. One
system. If the diagnosis is right, several other scores will move without you touching them
directly — and that lift, achieved without effort you spent, is the first real proof that you're
working structurally rather than by force.

## When to re-run this

Three triggers:

- **Quarterly, scheduled.** Non-negotiable, on the calendar, twenty minutes.
- **After any structural change.** New job, move, relationship change, health event, new business.
  The old snapshot describes a life you no longer have.
- **Whenever you feel the overload returning.** This is the reset button in the title. It is not a
  one-time intervention you either succeed or fail at. It is an instrument you pick up whenever the
  signal appears — and picking it up early, on purpose, is the entire difference between a reset and
  a collapse.

That last trigger is worth being explicit about, because it reframes what the audit is *for*. You
are not auditing because something has gone wrong. You are auditing so that you find out early
enough for the fix to be small.

## Failure modes

Four ways this goes wrong. Name them now so you catch yourself.

**Scoring the aspiration.** You rate Health a 4 because you know what good health requires and you
intend to get there. Score the last thirty days. Intention is not evidence.

**Auditing during a crisis.** Every score reads 1 or 2 in a bad week, and you'll conclude your
whole life is failing when actually one domain is on fire and you're standing too close. If you're
mid-crisis, audit anyway — but mark the snapshot `CRISIS` and re-run in thirty days before you build
anything off it.

**Finding five constraints.** If everything looks like the root cause, you haven't finished the
analysis. Force the choice. Being wrong about which single domain is the constraint costs you one
cycle. Refusing to choose costs you the whole method.

**Auditing instead of building.** The audit is comfortable. It feels like progress, it produces
tidy artifacts, and it asks nothing of you. Some people will run it three times before changing a
single thing. Ninety minutes, once, then move. If you catch yourself refining the instrument instead
of acting on the output, that avoidance *is* a finding — write it down.

## What you should have now

Before the next chapter, you should be holding:

1. A **decision load count**, with the Re-deciding number separated out
2. **Floor and Trend scores** for all five domains, each backed by evidence you could show someone
3. A **dated one-page snapshot**, somewhere visible
4. **One circled constraint** — the domain the next chapter goes to work on

If you have all four, you've done something most people never do: you've replaced a story about
your life with a measurement of it. Every system from here forward gets built against that
measurement rather than against a feeling.

If you skipped the exercises and read straight through — and some of you did — the next chapter will
be interesting and useless. It builds directly on the constraint you just didn't identify. Go back.
Ninety minutes.

You now know where the leak is. Chapter 4 is where you build the thing that plugs it.
